Tinkerbug Robotics Issue #491 Bench report 19 Aug 2026

Phoenix COCOM Gate

Does the SkyTraq PX1105R / PX1125R withhold position when altitude and speed are exceeded together, or whenever either one is? Measured on the bench: independently. Either one closes the gate on its own — and the altitude threshold is 80 km, not 18 km.

Answered · both limits measured

01Why it decides the receiver

Every GNSS receiver sold outside export control carries a gate that stops it producing position beyond a threshold of altitude and speed. What is not settled for the SkyTraq Phoenix engine is the operator between those two conditions.

A rocket almost never trips both at once. Boost is fast but low: past 515 m/s while still under 18 km. A space-shot apogee is high but slow: above 80 km with the vehicle nearly stopped. So if the gate is a true AND, it essentially never fires on a real flight, and the Phoenix parts become viable for high and fast work. If the two limits act independently, position disappears partway up the boost — the familiar, useless behaviour.

SkyTraq documents the AND reading in two of its own publications, neither of which names the Phoenix parts. The PX11xx datasheets state Operational Limits: Altitude < 80,000 m and velocity < 515 m/s, which can be read either way. The altitude figure is itself unresolved: 18 km in the FAQs, 80 km on the datasheet. Nothing published settles it, so it gets measured.

02How the signal is made

The receiver is fed a synthesised GPS L1 constellation carrying a trajectory we choose, injected by cable into its antenna port. Nothing is radiated: L1 is a live, protected band, and a transmitter at this power denies GPS to everything nearby, aircraft included.

Time comes from the signal, not the clock

The scenario is stamped with a start time, and a locked receiver reports that same UTC back in its GGA and RMC sentences. Trajectory time is therefore just NMEA UTC − scenario start — exact, and completely indifferent to when the operator pressed play. Host timestamps serve only as a fallback for epochs the receiver emits with no UTC field, and the offset between the two clocks is fitted across every epoch that carries both. The spread of that fit is reported with each capture; a spread beyond about two seconds means the playback underran and the run is discarded rather than interpreted.

Blocked is not the same as lost

The entire measurement rests on one distinction. A COCOM gate withholds position while the satellites stay tracked: C/N₀ holds at 40–45 dBHz and the GSV list is unchanged, but fix quality drops to zero and latitude and longitude go empty. A genuine loss of lock looks different — C/N₀ collapses and satellites fall out of view first. Classifying on GGA alone cannot tell them apart, so GSV is logged throughout and every epoch is scored three ways:

To keep that call unambiguous, the generator holds channel power constant for the whole run instead of modelling path loss. C/N₀ then stays flat from first sample to last, and any collapse is a real receiver behaviour rather than a modelled one. It is a deliberate trade of realism for diagnostic power.

The filed test 2 could not have worked

As originally specified, the altitude run climbed from 10 km to 85 km in 120 seconds. That is a vertical velocity of 625 m/s. COCOM acts on three-dimensional speed, so the climb alone would have carried the vehicle past the 515 m/s limit — the run would have exceeded both limits and settled nothing about an independent altitude gate.

The trajectories here are therefore defined by velocity functions that are integrated into position, never by altitude curves that are differentiated, and every scenario is checked against both limits before it is written out. The run table above prints what each one actually exceeds. The corrected t2 climbs at 350 m/s — 354 m/s in three dimensions, 69% of the limit — and crosses both candidate altitude gates in one continuous 240-second pass.

Continuity is doing real work

t2 starts at 5 km, where t0 and t1 have already proved the receiver fixes, and climbs without interruption through both candidate thresholds. That matters because a receiver that simply never acquires at 70 km is indistinguishable from one that is gated there. Beginning inside known-good territory and staying locked on the way up removes the ambiguity. t3b, which has no choice but to start high, opens with a 20-second static hold so a failure to acquire shows up as NO_LOCK from the first epoch rather than masquerading as a gate.

04Findings

The two limits act independently. Each was made to close the gate on its own, with the other nowhere near its threshold:

Velocity, at 5 km altitude. Three independent runs: normal 3-D fix at an injected 510 m/s, position withheld at 520 m/s, bracketing the documented 515 m/s. Altitude sat at 5.00 km throughout — below both candidate altitude gates. Under AND logic nothing should have happened.

Altitude, at 354 m/s. A slow climb held a fix continuously from 5 km straight through 18 km with no event whatsoever, and blanked between 79.55 km and 79.90 km. Speed never exceeded 354 m/s, 69% of the velocity limit.

Both transitions landed within about one second of where the trajectory crossed the limit — the 80 km crossing was predicted at t=464.3 s and observed between t=463 and t=464 — across two different scenarios probing two different quantities. That is not coincidence.

The 18 km figure is wrong for this engine, and the datasheet’s 80 km is right. More importantly, SkyTraq’s published claim that either limit may be exceeded alone is contradicted directly. For rocketry this is the unwelcome answer: these receivers will blank partway up a boost, because boost passes 515 m/s while still far below 80 km, and nothing about the altitude protects it.

Tracking is undisturbed; the receiver simply stops using what it is still receiving. A loss of signal looks nothing like this — satellites fall out first, which is exactly what the earlier drop-outs in the same runs did, and why the classifier scores those separately.

One loose end

Through the whole climb the receiver reported a frozen altitude of 4.94 km — close to the value it was seeded with — while reporting the climb velocity correctly at 354 m/s. It evidently knew its true altitude, since that is what it gated on, but did not publish it. Whether that is an artifact of the seeded warm restart or a reporting clamp in its own right is unresolved, and it matters for flight use independently of COCOM. It does not affect the gate measurement: the blanking point is set by the injected trajectory, and it landed on 80 km.

What it took to get a trustworthy run

Four things had to be fixed first, each of which produced a plausible wrong answer before it was found:

  • Seed the receiver, every run. Left alone it fought the injection for minutes — clock 618 s off, satellites tracked and ephemeris decoded but never used. A seeded warm restart (0x01 with the scenario’s time and position) cut time-to-first-fix to 43–63 s.
  • Give every scenario a stationary prologue. Time-to-first-fix ran 63–186 s, longer than the entire 90 s velocity ramp.
  • Less signal, not more. Solid lock at TX gain 22, never a fix at 32 — the front end overdrives. Higher C/N₀ with fewer satellites is the tell.
  • The classifier was under-counting satellites. The PX1125R reports one channel per signal, so a satellite appears several times in one 0xE7 — strong primary, near-zero secondary. Keying the C/N₀ map by satellite let the weak entry overwrite the strong one, turning a gate closure with 7 satellites held into an apparent collapse to 3. That is precisely the difference between BLOCKED and NO_LOCK.

05Recovery

Knowing where the gate closes says nothing about how long it stays shut. Answering that needs a trajectory that comes back under the limit, so vertical flights were flown, integrated from thrust, drag and gravity rather than drawn as ramps, and flown to the ground under a drogue-then-main recovery.

The gate re-opens in one epoch. On the gentle flight, the receiver descended back through 80 km at 217 m/s and had a fix again 0.7 s later, and the velocity gate re-opened in 1.1 s and 1.5 s in two other windows. Closing is just as sharp in the other direction: the fix disappeared between 0.3 s and 0.9 s after the limit was first exceeded, every time. This is an output inhibit, not a filter reset — which fits what the frames show, since the receiver never stops tracking across a gate transition.

A hypothesis that did not survive its own test

An earlier draft claimed a realistic 15 g boost breaks the receiver’s tracking loops — 15 g is 787 Hz/s of Doppler rate on L1, and the first two flights shed five of seven satellites within a second of ignition. The acceleration panel above was added to test that. It failed the test, twice.

Two independent runs, no satellite lost, 0 to 1334 m/s. The fix vanishing partway up the burn is the COCOM gate at 515 m/s, not a lock failure. On the 3 g flight the satellite count swung between two and seven while acceleration sat flat at −10 m/s², which acceleration cannot explain either. The claim is withdrawn.

So why does the satellite count move?

Not the injected signal: the generator transmits every satellite above the local horizon at equal, constant power for the whole run — elevation mask zero, path loss disabled — so about ten GPS satellites are present at full strength from first sample to last. And not signal strength either, which is the surprising part. Through a 146 s outage in the third run the satellites that were being tracked sat at 44 to 50 dBHz the whole time; there were just one to four of them, climbing by roughly one satellite every 30 to 40 seconds. That is the shape of sequential re-acquisition, not of a weak signal.

  • Too few satellites to lose any. This is the binding constraint. A fix needs four; the injected sky has ten and no second band or constellation behind them. Once the receiver drops a few it is under the threshold, and it climbs back one satellite at a time. Re-seating the SMA mid-session did not change this: median C/N₀ stayed at 44 dBHz and the run afterwards actually spent less of its time above four satellites, 70% against 89%.
  • Only ten satellites to lose. gps-sdr-sim is GPS L1 C/A only, while the receiver is multi-constellation and dual-band. In flight it would have GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and BeiDou across L1 and L5 — perhaps 25 to 35 signals, not 10. There is no redundancy in the injected sky, so a few losses take it under the four needed for a fix. The measured recovery times are pessimistic for this reason; the measured thresholds are not affected. Nor does picking a better hour help much: across the whole of 2026-08-19 the best offers twelve GPS satellites against the ten used.
  • Acceleration, if at all, only as a contributor. The measurement puts a ceiling on it: 677 Hz/s of Doppler rate cost nothing when the satellites had 15 dB of margin. It has not been shown to matter independently, and a bench holding a steady 49 dBHz would be needed to test it properly.

One earlier zero-satellite stretch was neither: it was a modelling error. The first version of the descent deployed the main parachute at 3 km while still doing 560 m/s, which produced about 7000 m/s² and, in an explicit integrator, reversed the sign of the velocity and lofted the vehicle back to 28 km. Both the flight and the tracking loss were artefacts. The profiles now fly a drogue from apogee and the main at 600 m AGL, landing at 6 m/s, and the drag term is clamped so it can bring a body to rest but never push it backwards.

06Receiver settings worth knowing about

The Phoenix protocol has no u-blox-style dynamics tuning. There is no exposed tracking-loop bandwidth and no Doppler search window; Configure Parameter Search Engine Number (0x64/0x0A) was removed from the protocol in a later revision, leaving only the query. Navigation mode is the only dynamics lever. Two things are nevertheless worth setting, and both are accepted by the bench firmware:

  • Power mode (0x0C, 0 = Normal). AN0037 states power save is enabled by default “to reduce current consumption by the search engine” — the same engine that has to re-acquire after a boost.
  • SLR, Speed Lag Reduced (0x64/0x17 mode 9), added in the same protocol revision as quadcopter mode, alongside airborne (mode 5).

Flown back to back on the 15 g profile against airborne with power save left at its default, neither changed anything that matters for the gate:

Acquisition, satellite count, signal level and fix availability are the same to within run-to-run scatter, and that scatter is large on this bench. The one column that moved is the one SLR is named for: reported speed compared against the injected speed with no time shift applied was 9.7 m/s RMS against 13.4 m/s. Allowing a best-fit shift collapses the difference — 7.9 against 7.5 m/s — so what changed is the delay in the velocity output, not its noise, which is exactly what “speed lag reduced” claims.

That is one run each, on a path whose C/N₀ has been seen to vary by 14 dB between runs. It is a lead, not a result. If velocity lag matters to the flight computer — and for apogee detection it does — it is worth repeating properly.

07What this cannot tell us

  • Still no vibration, and no multipath. The injected signal is clean. What the flight profiles added was real Doppler and real Doppler rate, which is why the 15 g runs lost tracking — but a real boost also brings vibration and airframe multipath on top of that, so the 15 g result here is a floor on the problem, not the whole of it. The dynamics work is tracked in #174, #249 and #262.
  • One receiver, one antenna port, one firmware. Tracking-loop bandwidth is a receiver-configuration matter as much as a silicon one; a different dynamic model setting might hold 15 g where this one did not. Only airborne mode was tried.
  • Navigation mode is a confound until it is pinned. The receiver's own dynamics filter can withhold position for reasons that have nothing to do with COCOM, and it produces the same NMEA symptom. Every run is made in a declared navigation mode — 0x64/0x17, airborne — and t1 is repeated in auto as a control. A difference between the two is itself a finding.
  • L1 only. The gate acts on the computed position solution, so the band it was computed from should be irrelevant. Should be. A dual-band confirmation run is cheap insurance if the L1 result is surprising.
  • One part, one firmware. Results bind to the silicon and firmware version on the bench, recorded with each capture. SkyTraq has changed this behaviour between engine generations before, which is the reason the published FAQs cannot be taken at face value here.
