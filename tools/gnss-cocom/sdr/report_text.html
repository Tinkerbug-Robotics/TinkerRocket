Hobby Grade GNSS Receiver Altitude and Speed Limits for High Performance Rockets

Eight hobby-grade GNSS receivers were flown against identical simulated rocket trajectories to find where each one stops publishing state updates. Receivers are typically limited to 'COCOM' limits which come from a Cold War international agreement to place limits on GNSS receivers and keep them from being used in ballistic missile guidance. Limits were originally 18 km (60,000 ft) and 515 m/s. The 18 km limit seems to have been removed more recently in favor of a 600 m/s limit from the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR Annex Handbook 2017, Section 11.A.3). Receivers still implement limits differently and generally still implement altitude limits, sometimes very different from the COCOM limit. The purpose of this work was to characterize the actual performance using a software-defined radio to inject simulated trajectories into several readily available inexpensive receivers.

What the table says

Receivers do not implement the current MTCR 600 m/s limit. Most use the COCOM 515 m/s limit for speed; the Quectel LC86G stops at 500 m/s, which matches neither. Altitude limits vary significantly from 10 km to 80 km, though several use the 80 km limit.

The LC86G appears twice because one setting changes the answer entirely. Its navigation mode lives in the module's RAM, and the Tinker-Beetle's firmware leaves it at the default, Normal. In Normal mode there is no altitude limit to report, because the fix is already wrong partway up the boost. In Balloon mode its limits are as clean as any part here. Section 04 sets the two side by side.

Limits combined

A receiver with both a speed and an altitude limit has to decide what to do when only one of them is exceeded. It can stop publishing as soon as either is passed, which the table calls independent, or only when both are passed at once, which would be an AND.

This decides whether a rocket loses position at all. A flight almost never trips both limits together: boost is fast but still low, and apogee is high but slow. A receiver using AND would therefore keep publishing through an entire sounding-rocket flight, because there is no moment where the vehicle is simultaneously above 80 km and above 515 m/s. Every part here that has both limits treats them independently, so every one of them blanks partway up the boost — tens of kilometers below its own altitude limit, with the flight barely started.

The Air530 is listed as not applicable because it has no speed limit to combine — only the altitude ceiling applies, so the question does not arise. The LC86G in Normal mode is not applicable for a different reason: no valid fix ever reaches either limit.

Re-open latency

How long the receiver takes to publish a position again after the vehicle drops back inside the limits. It is measured from the moment the trajectory clears the limit to the first valid fix that follows.

The shorter the delay, the faster you get fix back after passing below a threshold. Since the receivers continue to operate and have a fix internally when exceeding the limits, they should be able to report their fix quickly once inside the limits again. Four configurations recover in under 2 seconds: the PX1125R, SAM-M10Q and ZED-F9P, and the LC86G in Balloon mode, which comes back straight into a valid fix within 0.1 s on the 3 g flight. The NEO-M8T recovers within 3.1 s, and the two M10 modules take between 5 and 11 seconds. The LC86G in Normal mode never re-opened on the 3 g flight.

02How this was measured

A HackRF One drives a simulated GPS L1 C/A constellation into each receiver through a fixed attenuator chain. A direct path through cables is used whenever possible, with transmission only inside a Faraday cage — 100 dB of attenuation before the antenna, and monitoring outside the cage for GNSS interference. The HackRF only transmits one frequency at a time, so we only transmit L1 sats from the ephemeris. We found times/locations with a favorable number of available satellites (13-15). The GNSS signal comes from gps-sdr-sim against the real broadcast ephemeris, so the satellites are where they actually were on the day.

Blocked vs Lost

A receiver that has been blocked keeps tracking satellites and simply declines to publish a fix; a receiver that has lost signal loses the satellites first. Every capture records both the fix state and the per-satellite carrier-to-noise ratio, so the two can be told apart:

VerdictFixSatellitesMeaning
FIXyestrackedpublishing normally
BLOCKEDnostill trackedwithholding — a gate
NO_LOCKnonot trackedthe satellites were lost — not a gate
WRONGflagged validtrackedpublished, but more than 5 km or 50 m/s (vertical) from the truth
SILENTnothingunknownno output of any kind — the receiver muted itself

A result is only reported as a gate when the satellites stayed. Where a receiver was starved — fewer than four satellites through a wait — the analysis says so rather than quoting a latency. After working out bugs in the bench setup, this only came up in the Air530 case, which drops satellites on an 18 second period during a block.

The injected level is constant, so a satellite that disappears was lost by the receiver (or by a fault in the bench path), never by the signal fading. The last two states were added for the LC86G. It publishes fixes that are flagged valid but kilometres wrong (WRONG). And it enforces its limits by going completely quiet, dropping NMEA, acknowledgements and raw measurements alike (SILENT). The rig's bridge logs each silence as it happens, so a mute cannot be mistaken for a dead link.

The two standard trajectories

Every receiver flies both. They reach the same 82.5 km apogee and cross the same limits; only the boost differs, and that difference is the point.

ScenarioBoostPeak speedApogeePurpose
spaceshot15 g1343 m/s82.5 kmrealistic high-power flight; hardest case for tracking
gentle_alt3 g997 m/s82.5 kmsame envelope crossed slowly — this is what brackets the thresholds

Why two. At a 1 Hz navigation rate a 15 g boost covers 118 m/s between one epoch and the next, so a fast flight can only ever bracket the velocity gate coarsely no matter how carefully it is flown. The 3 g ascent crosses the same threshold slowly enough to pin it. Both are integrated from thrust, drag and gravity rather than drawn as ramps, and flown to the ground under a drogue-then-main recovery.

Each flight opens with a 180 s pad hold so the receiver has acquired and settled before anything moves. Trajectory time is recovered from the GPS time in the receiver's own output, so no clock synchronization between the transmitter and the analysis is needed.

One signal, one band. The injection is GPS L1 C/A only. Every receiver here is multi-constellation and two are multi-band, so all of them are being tested with fewer than typically visible satellites. That is a property of the bench setup, not of any receiver, but by picking favorable locations and times we ensured enough satellites were visible to characterize gate performance.

04Excursion tests

The two standard flights were enough for five of the eight parts. They were not enough for the Air530, which has no velocity limit at all and still had the worst performance. Unlike every other unit, the Air530 periodically drops the fix internally during times it is blocked — i.e. above 10 km. Two other parts stopped at ceilings that turned out not to be export gates: the NEO-M8T, whose ceiling is its dynamic model, and the Quectel LC86G, whose behavior depends on a navigation-mode setting.

This means that additional scenarios were needed to study the behavior of the Air530. So in addition to the data below, other scenarios were also run to confirm its altitude limit and lack of velocity limit.

What they established for the Air530

Three tests, three ways of asking the same question, all agreeing there is no velocity gate: 100% of epochs held a fix at every 90 s dwell from 495 to 530 m/s; a ramp to 900 m/s at 5 km never lost the fix and reported 899 m/s against 900 injected; and on blockdur the receiver held a fix at 560 m/s for 148 continuous seconds.

What it has instead is an altitude ceiling at 10–11 km. The dwell staircase is unambiguous — 100% of epochs fixed at 8 km, 97% at 9, 91% at 10, and not one epoch at 11, 12 or 13 km, with ten or eleven satellites tracked throughout — and two independent ramps agree (fix at 9.90 km, blocked at 10.25 km at a constant 354 m/s; fix at 10.10, blocked at 10.44 at 394 m/s).

That ceiling is not an export gate. It sits far below the COCOM altitude, and alt_stair_vlow crosses no export limit at all — 13 km and 156 m/s are well inside the envelope.

On a flight profile the ceiling and the velocity limit fire within moments of each other, because a rocket crosses 10 km while traveling fast. The distinguishing observation is on spaceshot, where the fix stops while speed is falling (1334 → 1304 m/s) as altitude rises through 9.83 km. No velocity gate fires on decreasing speed.

The same trap on the NEO-M8T

The M8T stops publishing at 50 km, which is the documented maximum altitude of u-blox's airborne dynamic models rather than an MTCR limit. The M8T has different models, and changing the model changed the limit, confirming that it was the model driving the limit and not MTCR. For example switching from airborne <4 g to portable moved the same ceiling from 49.80 km to 5.04 km.

No u-blox M8T model exceeds 50 km. The SAM-M10Q and ZED-F9P held fixes to 80 km, so it is an M8-generation behavior.

Normal vs Balloon: the Quectel LC86G

The eighth part is the GNSS on the Tinker-Beetle, a Quectel LC86G on the flight computer's own serial port. It was flown the way the Beetle runs it. The flight computer was loaded with a bench image that passes the module's serial output straight to USB, and the Beetle driver's own configuration was replayed through it: 10 Hz, the same sentences, nothing saved to the module. Then the same two flights were flown again with one setting changed.

That setting is the navigation mode. The Beetle's firmware never sends $PAIR080, so the module runs in its default, Normal. Quectel's protocol specification gives every mode but one a 10 km altitude limitation. It calls 10–50 km "cannot be guaranteed" and stops all output above 50 km. Balloon mode moves the limit to 80 km. The datasheet adds a 490 m/s maximum velocity and a 4 g maximum acceleration. This is the NEO-M8T's situation again, a ceiling set by a mode rather than by export control, and the test is the same: change the mode and watch what moves.

Normal mode cannot follow a boost. Through the first 18 s of the 3 g ascent it reported a climb rate of 1–6 m/s while the injection climbed to 320 m/s. All the while it reported a valid 3-D fix from 13 satellites. Balloon mode, on the same file, was within 2 m/s throughout:

Both modes stop all output at 500 m/s, measured on their own speed estimate. What happens next is the difference. Balloon mode came back within one 0.1 s epoch of the vehicle slowing below 500 m/s, straight into a valid fix at 69.5 km. It went quiet again above 80.0 km on its own altitude, and came back within an epoch on the way down. The two limits act independently: the speed limit fired at 9.3 km, the altitude limit at 173 m/s. That is the same shape as every other part here, but enforced by silence rather than by withholding the position.

Normal mode never published a valid fix again after its mute. That covers 580 s of descent, most of it slow under a drogue, on the same signal the module tracked at 45 dBHz in Balloon mode. The likeliest reason is its own state. Having never believed the climb, it would have predicted each satellite's Doppler kilohertz away from the truth when it tried to reacquire. That is an inference from the pattern, not a measurement.

At 15 g both modes lose every satellite at ignition (section 06). Normal mode then published valid-flagged fixes 18–80 km wrong: 1.3 km at apogee against 82 km, and −5 km against +13 km on the descent. It then withheld, with 13 satellites tracked, until the main parachute opened. Balloon mode published nothing wrong. It had no fix until the descent brought the vehicle back under 500 m/s, then a valid one within 0.6 s.

Three details matter to anyone reading this part's output. Its UTC runs 18 s behind GPS time, because it applies leap seconds the simulation never broadcast; the analysis uses $PQTMPVT's GPS time of week instead. Through the 15 g coast its $GNGGA claimed a three-satellite fix that $PQTMPVT reported as no fix. The analysis follows $PQTMPVT, which is what the Beetle's driver reads. And on this bench its altitude drifts low through a flight, 2.6 km by landing, with velocity still right to 0.5 m/s. A u-blox M10 drifts 1.5 km on the same file, so most of that is the bench, not the receiver. The drift is also why the Balloon-mode altitude limit reads near 81 km against the injection while the receiver itself stops at 80.0.

05A receiver-side artifact

The Air530's satellite count dips on a tight 18 s cycle. Ruling out bench issues here was important, since loose connectors or other bench artifacts can drive errors. Three tests placed this behavior on the Air530 and not the bench though.

A control part on the same path shows none of it. The ZED-F9P ran the identical scenario files through the identical 70 dB conducted chain with zero periodic dips. Every satellite blanks in the same epoch and returns together within three — attenuation is graded, so a real signal problem takes the weakest first; instead all twelve go from a flat 49 dBHz to an empty C/N₀ field at once, which is a reporting decision, not a level. And it happens while the receiver is withholding: 19 of 677 such epochs on gentle_alt against 0 of 170 while publishing, and on blockdur, where it holds a fix almost throughout, 2 events in 988 epochs.

18 s is three GPS subframes, the span of subframes 1–3 that carry the ephemeris, so the part may re-validate ephemeris on that cadence and blank C/N₀ while doing so. That is a hypothesis; the three observations above stand without it.

06What the boost drops

Everything above is about the export gate: the moment a receiver refuses to publish a position it is perfectly capable of computing. This section is about a different kind of loss, and one that is not a policy decision at all. Through the burn the u-blox receivers keep publishing, but each one stops tracking some of its satellites — and which ones it loses is not random. The eighth part, the LC86G, does not keep publishing. It is taken up at the end of this section.

Doppler rate is the suspect. An L1 carrier shifts 5.25 Hz for every m/s of range rate, so under acceleration the shift is not merely large, it is moving, and the tracking loop has to slew to follow it. How fast it moves depends on where the satellite sits relative to the velocity vector, which for a vertical boost means it scales with elevation. That gives two candidate explanations worth separating, because they are different failures: the carrier moving too fast for the loop, or the carrier simply sitting too far off nominal.

Both quantities can be measured rather than modeled. The NEO-M8T logged RXM-RAWX, which reports the receiver's own Doppler per satellite in hertz, and every flight replayed a byte-identical scenario file — same ephemeris, same start time, same trajectory — so the Doppler a given satellite presents is a property of the injected signal rather than of the receiver listening to it. The same numbers therefore apply to every part flown on it. What follows is measured Doppler, not elevation standing in for it.

Carrier loss scales with the rate. Pooling both flights onto one axis makes the gentle one the low end of a single curve rather than a separate condition: its satellites never exceed 79 Hz/s and never lose carrier, while the same satellites on the same sky reach 501 Hz/s under a real boost and shed 15 to 47 dB. Above 300 Hz/s the median satellite is down 14 dB; below 100 Hz/s the median is unchanged.

And it is the rate, not the shift. Peak shift is a genuine control here rather than a straw man, because it is close to independent of elevation — it is set mostly by the satellite's own motion, and it ranges from 1.2 to 6.9 kHz across this sky with no relation to how hard the loop is working. GPS 5 sits at 28° carrying the largest shift of any satellite, 6915 Hz, and barely suffers. On every one of the four the correlation against rate is the stronger one, and on the ZED-F9P the shift explains essentially nothing:

The published limit is a g number for a Hz/s problem. u-blox rates the M10 to 4 g, but a loop does not see acceleration, it sees how fast its carrier is sliding — and the same 4 g produces 206 Hz/s on a satellite at zenith and 71 Hz/s on one at 20°. One acceleration is therefore not one condition; it is a spread. That is visible in the flight above, which holds 13.5 g, 3.4 times the rated limit, throughout: split its satellites at the 4 g-equivalent rate and the ones below it are down 4 dB while the ones above are down 10 dB — same vehicle, same instant, differing only in where they sit in the sky. A part flown past its rated acceleration does not fail all at once, and a part kept under it is not automatically safe if something is overhead.

So the mechanism has a name: the tracking loop failing to slew, not the carrier sitting far off nominal. Elevation, which this section used to argue from, turns out to have been a sound proxy rather than a wrong one — the measured rate matches a × sin(elevation) × 5.25 to within a factor of 0.82 to 0.98 across twelve satellites, the residual being the difference between peak and mean acceleration over the burn. That is why it worked.

The clearest single observation is a satellite that disappears. GPS 11 sits at 70°, the highest in this sky and the steepest Doppler rate of any satellite in the set. Its raw measurement holds steady at −98 Hz on the pad; one second after ignition it reads +483 Hz and its carrier has fallen from 48 to 30 dBHz; then it vanishes from the raw output entirely for ten seconds, and returns at +6302 Hz with its carrier back at 47 dBHz the moment the burn ends. It is bracketed by the acceleration on both sides. It is also, for that reason, the one satellite absent from the rate axis above: the instrument that would measure the stimulus is the one the stimulus disabled.

What actually dropped. Of these four, only the ZED-F9P lost satellites outright, three of thirteen: GPS 11 at 70°, GPS 24 at 51° and GPS 6 at 31°, each from a healthy carrier to nothing. The other three receivers leaned on the same satellites — 12 to 32 dB off the two steepest — but held lock on all of them. The ranking is consistent across parts even where the outcome is not.

What did not drop, on these four, is the position. None of them went to no-lock during any burn. Losing three satellites out of thirteen costs geometry, not a fix. On the u-blox parts, the thing that stops a position is the gate, not the dynamics.

Two caveats carry real weight. The four parts behind the curve above are all u-blox, spanning three generations (M8, M10, F9) but one vendor. The LC86G below is the first part from another vendor, and it fails differently. And the NEO-M8T reports a flat 51 dBHz for all thirteen satellites on the pad, so its carrier is clamped at this injection level and its correlations should be read as a ranking of burn carrier rather than a change from a measured baseline. What makes the result credible is four receivers agreeing, the effect scaling with acceleration, and the rate beating the shift on every one of them.

The eighth part loses the position. The LC86G reports its raw measurements as RTCM MSM7: per-satellite C/N₀, its own Doppler, and a carrier-phase lock counter, once a second. It was flown on the same byte-identical files, so each of its channels can be set against the Doppler rate the NEO-M8T measured for the same satellite. It fails in two stages. At the 13.5 g ignition every channel drops within the first second, low and high alike. That is a common-mode failure, not per-channel stress, and it happens in both navigation modes. Then the rate-limited picture reasserts itself. The channels whose Doppler rate is low relock within two seconds and hold through the rest of the burn, and the others do not.

The knee sits between 128 and 171 Hz/s. That is below the 206 Hz/s its rated 4 g would present straight overhead, so a satellite near zenith is lost somewhere around 3 g of sustained acceleration. With only the four lowest satellites left, all under 13°, it has no fix for the rest of the burn and coast. The 2 g flight sits under the knee everywhere, and the channels show it. All thirteen held 46 dBHz through the burn, and only the three steepest, at 79, 66 and 53 Hz/s, slipped carrier phase at ignition. That is the same ranking the u-blox parts show in carrier loss, visible here in phase, at an acceleration too low to cost any carrier at all.

So the LC86G is the one part on this bench whose fix a 15 g boost costs. It isn't the export gate, which it never reached; it's the dynamics alone, at ignition. On a vehicle that boosts that hard it will not have a position until the descent, in either mode.

One boundary is worth stating plainly, because it decides how far this generalizes. The rig injects smooth, clean, interpolated motion. A real motor adds broadband vibration that modulates carrier phase directly, plume attenuation, body shadowing and antenna-pattern nulls as the vehicle rolls, and a signal level well below the 32–51 dBHz delivered here — and loop stress multiplies with low carrier. A loop that holds 500 Hz/s at 45 dBHz can fail at 32. This bench characterizes the export gate; a part that sails through boost here may still drop lock on a real motor, and nothing measured here contradicts that.
