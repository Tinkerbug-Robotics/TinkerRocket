Hobby Grade GNSS Receiver Altitude and Speed Limits for High Performance Rockets

Eight hobby-grade GNSS receivers were flown against identical simulated rocket trajectories to find where each one stops publishing state updates. Receivers are typically limited to 'COCOM' limits which come from a Cold War international agreement to place limits on GNSS receivers and keep them from being used in ballistic missile guidance. Limits were originally 18 km (60,000 ft) and 515 m/s. The 18 km limit seems to have been removed more recently in favor of a 600 m/s limit from the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR Annex Handbook 2017, Section 11.A.3). Receivers still implement limits differently and generally still implement altitude limits, sometimes very different from the COCOM limit. The purpose of this work was to characterize the actual performance using a software-defined radio to inject simulated trajectories into several readily available inexpensive receivers.

Eight parts were measured

Every receiver here was flown against the same two trajectories through the same signal generator. What the boost itself does to tracking, on the same flights, is in the companion report, GNSS Under Boost.

What the table says

Receivers do not implement the current MTCR 600 m/s limit. Most use the COCOM 515 m/s limit for speed; the Quectel LC86G stops at 500 m/s, which matches neither. Altitude limits vary significantly from 10 km to 80 km, though several use the 80 km limit. The Air530's 10 km and the NEO-M8T's 50 km are not export limits; section 04 shows why.

The LC86G is listed in Balloon mode, one of its navigation modes. The module powers up in Normal mode, and the setting lives in its RAM, so a host has to select Balloon again at every power-up. In Normal mode there is no altitude limit to report, because the fix is already wrong partway up the boost. Drone mode follows the boost, but once it reached 500 m/s it never published a fix again. Section 04 sets the three side by side.

Limits combined

A receiver with both a speed and an altitude limit has to decide what to do when only one of them is exceeded. It can stop publishing as soon as either is passed, which the table calls independent, or only when both are passed at once, which would be an AND.

This decides whether a rocket loses position at all. A flight almost never trips both limits together: boost is fast but still low, and apogee is high but slow. A receiver using AND would therefore keep publishing through an entire sounding-rocket flight, because there is no moment where the vehicle is simultaneously above 80 km and above 515 m/s. Every part here that has both limits treats them independently, so every one of them blanks partway up the boost — tens of kilometers below its own altitude limit, with the flight barely started.

The Air530 is listed as not applicable because it has no speed limit to combine — only the altitude ceiling applies, so the question does not arise. Section 06 keeps the runs that first established independence on the PX1125R, one limit at a time.

Re-open latency

How long the receiver takes to publish a position again after the vehicle drops back inside the limits. It is measured from the moment the trajectory clears the limit to the first valid fix that follows.

The shorter the delay, the faster you get fix back after passing below a threshold. Since the receivers continue to operate and have a fix internally when exceeding the limits, they should be able to report their fix quickly once inside the limits again. Four parts recover in under 2 seconds: the PX1125R, SAM-M10Q, ZED-F9P and LC86G, which comes back straight into a valid fix within 0.1 s on the 3 g flight. The NEO-M8T recovers within 3.1 s, and the Quescan M10 and Beitian BN-182 take between 5 and 11 seconds.

02How this was measured

A HackRF One drives a simulated GPS L1 C/A constellation into each receiver through a fixed attenuator chain. A direct path through cables is used whenever possible, with transmission only inside a Faraday cage — 100 dB of attenuation before the antenna, and monitoring outside the cage for GNSS interference. The HackRF only transmits one frequency at a time, so we only transmit L1 sats from the ephemeris. We found times/locations with a favorable number of available satellites (13-15). The GNSS signal comes from gps-sdr-sim against the real broadcast ephemeris, so the satellites are where they actually were on the day.

Blocked vs Lost

A receiver that has been blocked keeps tracking satellites and simply declines to publish a fix; a receiver that has lost signal loses the satellites first. Every capture records both the fix state and the per-satellite carrier-to-noise ratio, so the two can be told apart:

VerdictFixSatellitesMeaning
FIXyestrackedpublishing normally
BLOCKEDnostill trackedwithholding — a gate
NO_LOCKnonot trackedthe satellites were lost — not a gate
WRONGflagged validtrackedpublished, but more than 5 km or 50 m/s (vertical) from the truth
SILENTnothingunknownno output of any kind — the receiver muted itself

A result is only reported as a gate when the satellites stayed. Where a receiver was starved — fewer than four satellites through a wait — the analysis says so rather than quoting a latency. After working out bugs in the bench setup, this only came up in the Air530 case, which drops satellites on an 18 second period during a block.

The injected level is constant, so a satellite that disappears was lost by the receiver (or by a fault in the bench path), never by the signal fading. The last two states were added for the LC86G. It publishes fixes that are flagged valid but kilometres wrong (WRONG). And it enforces its limits by going completely quiet, dropping NMEA, acknowledgements and raw measurements alike (SILENT). The rig's bridge logs each silence as it happens, so a mute cannot be mistaken for a dead link.

The two standard trajectories

Every receiver flies both. They reach the same 82.5 km apogee and cross the same limits; only the boost differs, and that difference is the point.

ScenarioBoostPeak speedApogeePurpose
spaceshot15 g1343 m/s82.5 kmrealistic high-power flight; hardest case for tracking
gentle_alt3 g997 m/s82.5 kmsame envelope crossed slowly — this is what brackets the thresholds

Why two. Six of the eight parts ran at 1 Hz, and the SAM-M10Q, though it ran at 18 Hz, was logged once a second; only the LC86G's 10 Hz reached the analysis. At 1 Hz a 15 g boost covers 118 m/s between one epoch and the next, so a fast flight can only ever bracket the velocity gate coarsely no matter how carefully it is flown. The 3 g ascent crosses the same threshold slowly enough to pin it. Both are integrated from thrust, drag and gravity rather than drawn as ramps, and flown to the ground under a drogue-then-main recovery.

Each flight opens with a 180 s pad hold so the receiver has acquired and settled before anything moves. Trajectory time is recovered from the GPS time in the receiver's own output, so no clock synchronization between the transmitter and the analysis is needed.

One signal, one band. The injection is GPS L1 C/A only. Every receiver here is multi-constellation and two are multi-band, so all of them are being tested with fewer than typically visible satellites. That is a property of the bench setup, not of any receiver, but by picking favorable locations and times we ensured enough satellites were visible to characterize gate performance.

04Excursion tests

The two standard flights were enough for five of the eight parts. They were not enough for the Air530, which has no velocity limit at all and still had the worst performance. Unlike every other unit, the Air530 periodically drops the fix internally during times it is blocked — i.e. above 10 km. Two other parts stopped at ceilings that turned out not to be export gates: the NEO-M8T, whose ceiling is its dynamic model, and the Quectel LC86G, whose behavior depends on a navigation-mode setting.

This means that additional scenarios were needed to study the behavior of the Air530. So in addition to the data below, other scenarios were also run to confirm its altitude limit and lack of velocity limit.

What they established for the Air530

Three tests, three ways of asking the same question, all agreeing there is no velocity gate: 100% of epochs held a fix at every 90 s dwell from 495 to 530 m/s; a ramp to 900 m/s at 5 km never lost the fix and reported 899 m/s against 900 injected; and on blockdur the receiver held a fix at 560 m/s for 148 continuous seconds.

What it has instead is an altitude ceiling at 10–11 km. The dwell staircase is unambiguous — 100% of epochs fixed at 8 km, 97% at 9, 91% at 10, and not one epoch at 11, 12 or 13 km, with ten or eleven satellites tracked throughout — and two independent ramps agree (fix at 9.90 km, blocked at 10.25 km at a constant 354 m/s; fix at 10.10, blocked at 10.44 at 394 m/s).

That ceiling is not an export gate. It sits far below the COCOM altitude, and alt_stair_vlow crosses no export limit at all — 13 km and 156 m/s are well inside the envelope.

On a flight profile the ceiling and the velocity limit fire within moments of each other, because a rocket crosses 10 km while traveling fast. The distinguishing observation is on spaceshot, where the fix stops while speed is falling (1334 → 1304 m/s) as altitude rises through 9.83 km. No velocity gate fires on decreasing speed.

The same trap on the NEO-M8T

The M8T stops publishing at 50 km, which is the documented maximum altitude of u-blox's airborne dynamic models rather than an MTCR limit. The M8T has different models, and changing the model changed the limit, confirming that it was the model driving the limit and not MTCR. For example switching from airborne <4 g to portable moved the same ceiling from 49.80 km to 5.04 km.

No u-blox M8T model exceeds 50 km. The SAM-M10Q and ZED-F9P held fixes to 80 km, so it is an M8-generation behavior.

Normal, Balloon and Drone: the Quectel LC86G

The eighth part, a Quectel LC86G, was flown on a rocket flight computer board, through a bench image that passes the module's serial output straight to USB. It was configured the way a flight driver configures it: 10 Hz, the position sentences a flight computer reads, nothing saved to the module. The two standard flights were flown three times, once in each of three navigation modes.

The navigation mode is set with $PAIR080. The module powers up in Normal mode, so a host that never sends the command flies in Normal, and the setting lives in RAM, so it has to be sent again at every power-up. Quectel's protocol specification gives every mode but one a 10 km altitude limitation. It calls 10–50 km "cannot be guaranteed" and stops all output above 50 km. Balloon mode moves the limit to 80 km. The datasheet adds a 490 m/s maximum velocity and a 4 g maximum acceleration. This is the NEO-M8T's situation again, a ceiling set by a mode rather than by export control, and the test is the same: change the mode and watch what moves.

The third mode flown is Drone. Older Quectel parts offered an Aviation mode as mode 2; this specification marks mode 2 reserved, and the firmware refuses it with a parameter error. Drone mode is the one the specification describes for vertical acceleration, and it keeps the 10 km limitation. Its two flights were made on a later day with the signal about 3 dB weaker at the receiver, 42 against 45 dBHz on the pad, at the same transmit gain.

Normal mode cannot follow a boost. Through the first 18 s of the 3 g ascent it reported a climb rate of 1–6 m/s while the injection climbed to 320 m/s. All the while it reported a valid 3-D fix from 13 satellites. Balloon and Drone modes, on the same file, lagged only in the first two seconds after liftoff, by up to 12 and 35 m/s, and then held within 2 m/s to the mute:

Drone mode's altitude falls further behind than Balloon's, 0.45 km low at 210 s against 0.24 km, because it is about a second old. Its climb rate is current, but its altitude matches the injection of 0.98 s earlier, to 11 m rms over the boost. At 500 m/s that second is 500 m.

All three modes stop all output at 500 m/s, measured on their own speed estimate. What happens next is the difference. Balloon mode came back within one 0.1 s epoch of the vehicle slowing below 500 m/s, straight into a valid fix at 69.5 km. It went quiet again above 80.0 km on its own altitude, and came back within an epoch on the way down. The two limits act independently: the speed limit fired at 9.3 km, the altitude limit at 173 m/s. That is the same shape as every other part here, but enforced by silence rather than by withholding the position.

Normal mode never published a valid fix again after its mute. That covers 580 s of descent, most of it slow under a drogue, on the same signal the module tracked at 45 dBHz in Balloon mode. The likeliest reason is its own state. Having never believed the climb, it would have predicted each satellite's Doppler kilohertz away from the truth when it tried to reacquire. That is an inference from the pattern, not a measurement.

Drone mode did not recover either, and it failed differently. It had followed the climb, and it kept its channels: from the end of its mute to landing it tracked a median of 13 satellites at 42 dBHz, where Normal mode held a median of four. Yet every position report it sent over those ten minutes says no fix and no satellites used, long after the vehicle was back inside both of the mode's limits, under 500 m/s from 291 s and under 10 km from 597 s. The tracking came back; the solution never did.

At 15 g all three modes lose every satellite at ignition (GNSS Under Boost has the channel-by-channel picture). Normal mode then published valid-flagged fixes 18–80 km wrong: 1.3 km at apogee against 82 km, and −5 km against +13 km on the descent. It then withheld, with 13 satellites tracked, until the main parachute opened. Balloon mode published nothing wrong. It had no fix until the descent brought the vehicle back under 500 m/s, then a valid one within 0.6 s. Drone mode published nothing wrong either, and nothing at all for longer. Its raw measurements carry no channel from ignition until 242 s, 50 s after burnout, and though it tracked a median of 8 satellites from 260 s, its first fix came at 571.4 s, 3.7 s after the vehicle descended through 10 km, the mode's documented limit. That fix was right.

Three details matter to anyone reading this part's output. Its UTC runs 18 s behind GPS time, because it applies leap seconds the simulation never broadcast; the analysis uses $PQTMPVT's GPS time of week instead. Through the 15 g coast its $GNGGA claimed a three-satellite fix that $PQTMPVT reported as no fix. The analysis follows $PQTMPVT, the sentence a flight computer reads for its fix. And on this bench its altitude drifts low through a flight, 2.6 km by landing, with velocity still right to 0.5 m/s. The Quescan M10 drifts 1.5 km on the same file, so most of that is the bench, not the receiver. The drift is also why the Balloon-mode altitude limit reads near 81 km against the injection while the receiver itself stops at 80.0.

05A receiver-side artifact

The Air530's satellite count dips on a tight 18 s cycle. Ruling out bench issues here was important, since loose connectors or other bench artifacts can drive errors. Three tests placed this behavior on the Air530 and not the bench though.

A control part on the same path shows none of it. The ZED-F9P ran the identical scenario files through the identical 70 dB conducted chain with zero periodic dips. Every satellite blanks in the same epoch and returns together within three — attenuation is graded, so a real signal problem takes the weakest first; instead all twelve go from a flat 49 dBHz to an empty C/N₀ field at once, which is a reporting decision, not a level. And it happens while the receiver is withholding: 19 of 677 such epochs on gentle_alt against 0 of 170 while publishing, and on blockdur, where it holds a fix almost throughout, 2 events in 988 epochs.

18 s is three GPS subframes, the span of subframes 1–3 that carry the ephemeris, so the part may re-validate ephemeris on that cadence and blank C/N₀ while doing so. That is a hypothesis; the three observations above stand without it.

06Appendix: the first receiver, one limit at a time

This report began on 19–20 August as one question about one part: does the SkyTraq PX1125R withhold position when altitude and speed are exceeded together, or whenever either one is? The runs that answered it came before the standard flights of section 02. Each moved a single variable on a cabled bench, and together they are the cleanest evidence behind the independent entries in section 01. They were first published as a report of their own, and are kept here with the lessons that still govern how the rig is run.

SkyTraq documents the AND reading in two of its own publications, neither of which names the Phoenix parts. The PX11xx datasheets state Operational Limits: Altitude < 80,000 m and velocity < 515 m/s, which can be read either way, and the altitude figure was itself unsettled: 18 km in the FAQs, 80 km on the datasheet. Nothing published settled it, so it was measured.

The filed test 2 could not have worked

As originally specified, the altitude run climbed from 10 km to 85 km in 120 seconds. That is a vertical velocity of 625 m/s. COCOM acts on three-dimensional speed, so the climb alone would have carried the vehicle past the 515 m/s limit — the run would have exceeded both limits and settled nothing about an independent altitude gate.

The trajectories are therefore defined by velocity functions that are integrated into position, never by altitude curves that are differentiated, and every scenario is checked against both limits before it is written out. The run table above prints what each one actually exceeds. The corrected t2 climbs at 350 m/s — 354 m/s in three dimensions, 69% of the limit — and crosses both candidate altitude gates in one continuous 240-second pass.

Continuity is doing real work

t2 starts at 5 km, where t0 and t1 have already proved the receiver fixes, and climbs without interruption through both candidate thresholds. That matters because a receiver that simply never acquires at 70 km is indistinguishable from one that is gated there. Beginning inside known-good territory and staying locked on the way up removes the ambiguity. t3b, which has no choice but to start high, opens with a 20-second static hold so a failure to acquire shows up as NO_LOCK from the first epoch rather than masquerading as a gate.

What the runs found

Velocity, at 5 km altitude. Three independent runs: normal 3-D fix at an injected 510 m/s, position withheld at 520 m/s, bracketing the documented 515 m/s. Altitude sat at 5.00 km throughout — below both candidate altitude gates. Under AND logic nothing should have happened.

Altitude, at 354 m/s. A slow climb held a fix continuously from 5 km straight through 18 km with no event whatsoever, and blanked between 79.55 km and 79.90 km. Speed never exceeded 354 m/s, 69% of the velocity limit.

Both transitions landed within about one second of where the trajectory crossed the limit — the 80 km crossing was predicted at t=464.3 s and observed between t=463 and t=464 — across two different scenarios probing two different quantities. That is not coincidence.

The 18 km figure is wrong for this engine, and the datasheet's 80 km is right. SkyTraq's published claim that either limit may be exceeded alone is contradicted directly, with the consequence section 01 draws: the gate closes partway up a boost, because boost passes 515 m/s far below 80 km and the altitude does not protect it.

One loose end

Through the whole climb the receiver reported a frozen altitude of 4.94 km — close to the value it was seeded with — while reporting the climb velocity correctly at 354 m/s. It evidently knew its true altitude, since that is what it gated on, but did not publish it. Whether that is an artifact of the seeded warm restart or a reporting clamp in its own right is unresolved, and it matters for flight use independently of COCOM. It does not affect the gate measurement: the blanking point is set by the injected trajectory, and it landed on 80 km.

What it took to get a trustworthy run

Four things had to be fixed first, each of which produced a plausible wrong answer before it was found:

  • Seed the receiver, every run. Left alone it fought the injection for minutes — clock 618 s off, satellites tracked and ephemeris decoded but never used. A seeded warm restart (0x01 with the scenario's time and position) cut time-to-first-fix to 43–63 s.
  • Give every scenario a stationary prologue. Time-to-first-fix ran 63–186 s, longer than the entire 90 s velocity ramp. The 180 s pad hold on every flight in section 02 comes from this.
  • Less signal, not more. Solid lock at TX gain 22, never a fix at 32 — the front end overdrives. Higher C/N₀ with fewer satellites is the tell.
  • The classifier was under-counting satellites. The PX1125R reports one channel per signal, so a satellite appears several times in one 0xE7 — strong primary, near-zero secondary. Keying the C/N₀ map by satellite let the weak entry overwrite the strong one, turning a gate closure with 7 satellites held into an apparent collapse to 3. That is precisely the difference between BLOCKED and NO_LOCK.

The cabled bench

The PX1125R was fed by cable, from a PortaPack playing the scenario off its SD card. Nothing was radiated: L1 is a live, protected band, and a transmitter at this power denies GPS to everything nearby, aircraft included. Where a later part had to be radiated, it was inside the sealed cage described in section 02.

Recovery: the gate re-opens in about an epoch

Knowing where the gate closes says nothing about how long it stays shut, and the matched pair in section 03 answered that. On the gentle flight the receiver descended back through 80 km at 217 m/s and had a fix again 0.7 s later. Closing is just as sharp in the other direction: the fix disappeared between 0.3 and 0.9 s after a limit was first exceeded, every time. This is an output inhibit, not a filter reset, which fits what the frames show: the receiver never stops tracking across a gate transition.

A hypothesis that did not survive its own test

An early draft claimed a realistic 15 g boost breaks the receiver's tracking loops — 15 g is 787 Hz/s of Doppler rate on L1 at zenith, and the first two flights shed five of seven satellites within a second of ignition. An acceleration panel was added to the plots to test that. The claim failed the test, twice:

Two independent runs, no satellite lost, 0 to 1334 m/s. The fix vanishing partway up the burn is the gate at 515 m/s, not a lock failure; on the 3 g flight the satellite count swung between two and seven while acceleration sat flat, which acceleration cannot explain either. What acceleration does to tracking was left open here. The companion report, GNSS Under Boost, answers it on parts that report raw Doppler: it is the Doppler rate at each satellite that matters, so the loss depends on where a satellite sits in the sky. No raw Doppler was logged from the PX1125R, so it is not part of that analysis.

So why did the satellite count move?

Not the injected signal, which transmits every visible satellite at equal, constant power for the whole run, and not the trajectory, which was the obvious suspect twice over. The cabled chain carried a periodic oscillation in reported C/N₀ of about 15 dB, with a period near 80 s, and every satellite-count dip in every flight sat in one of its troughs. In the two matched flights the epochs that fell below four satellites sat 14 dB and 38 dB below their own run's median C/N₀. It was present under every condition tested, which is how it was cornered:

What remained was the receiver's own AGC or C/N₀ estimator, or something subtler in the chain. It was localized but never identified. The radiated setup used for the later parts does not show it. None of it touches the gate results: every threshold and every recovery latency was measured across a transition where the satellites were tracked either side, which is the condition the classifier requires before it will call anything at all.

Too few satellites to lose any

A fix needs four, and the first sky had ten. Re-seating the SMA changed nothing: median C/N₀ stayed at 44 dBHz, and the run afterwards spent less of its time above four satellites, 70% against 89%. Moving the simulated launch site to the equator and the scenario to 08:30, against a complete-day ephemeris, put fourteen in the sky instead, and that changed everything:

It is redundancy: with ten satellites the receiver is one bad moment from dropping under the threshold, and with fourteen it is not. That is where section 02's choice of times and places with 13–15 satellites comes from. With L1 GPS only, recovery times on this bench are pessimistic; the thresholds are not affected.

The simulator was hiding satellites at altitude

gps-sdr-sim decides visibility against the local horizontal plane. From height h the horizon is depressed by acos(R/(R+h)) — 9.2° at 82.5 km — so a vehicle up there genuinely sees a band of satellites the simulator was withholding. Worse, allocateChannel accepts an elvMask parameter and ignores it, calling checkSatVisibility with a hardcoded 0.0, so passing a correct mask in changes nothing until that is fixed too. With both repaired (patch_horizon.py) the 40°N site transmits 14 satellites at 82.5 km against 11 on the ground, and the receiver tracked a median of 8 rather than 7.

A modeling error that looked like a tracking loss

One early zero-satellite stretch was neither the gate nor the bench. The first version of the descent deployed the main parachute at 3 km while still doing 560 m/s, which produced about 7000 m/s² and, in an explicit integrator, reversed the sign of the velocity and lofted the vehicle back to 28 km. The flight and the tracking loss were both artifacts. The profiles now fly a drogue from apogee and the main at 600 m AGL, landing at 6 m/s, and the drag term is clamped so it can bring a body to rest but never push it backwards.

Receiver settings worth knowing about

The Phoenix protocol has no u-blox-style dynamics tuning. There is no exposed tracking-loop bandwidth and no Doppler search window; Configure Parameter Search Engine Number (0x64/0x0A) was removed from the protocol in a later revision, leaving only the query. Navigation mode is the only dynamics lever, and every PX1125R run was made in a declared one, airborne (0x64/0x17 mode 5), because a receiver's own dynamics filter can withhold position for reasons that have nothing to do with COCOM and produce the same output. Section 04 shows that confound deciding everything on the LC86G. Two other settings are worth knowing, and the bench firmware accepts both:

  • Power mode (0x0C, 0 = Normal). AN0037 states power save is enabled by default “to reduce current consumption by the search engine” — the same engine that has to re-acquire after a boost.
  • SLR, Speed Lag Reduced (0x64/0x17 mode 9), added in the same protocol revision as quadcopter mode, alongside airborne (mode 5).

Flown back to back on the 15 g profile against airborne with power save left at its default, neither changed anything that matters for the gate:

Acquisition, satellite count, signal level and fix availability are the same to within run-to-run scatter, and that scatter was large on this bench. The one column that moved is the one SLR is named for: reported speed compared against the injected speed with no time shift applied was 9.7 m/s RMS against 13.4 m/s. Allowing a best-fit shift collapses the difference — 7.9 against 7.5 m/s — so what changed is the delay in the velocity output, not its noise, which is exactly what “speed lag reduced” claims. That is one run each, on a path whose C/N₀ was seen to vary by 14 dB between runs: a lead, not a result. If velocity lag matters to the flight computer — and for apogee detection it does — it is worth repeating properly.

Results bind to the silicon and firmware on the bench, recorded with each capture. SkyTraq has changed this behavior between engine generations before, which is why its published FAQs could not be taken at face value.
