Bench report 29 Sep 2026

How Much Signal a GNSS Receiver Needs to Hold On Through a Boost

Two receivers flew the same two simulated space-shot boosts again and again while the signal was stepped down, 3 dB at a time, until they gave out. The SkyTraq PX1105R starts losing satellites early in the burn at every level a real sky delivers, and how early is set by how hard each satellite's carrier is sliding and how strong it is. The u-blox NEO-M8T loses nothing before its own 515 m/s cutoff until the signal is weaker than any open sky would give it, and holds carriers sliding at 1,400 Hz a second on a 40 g boost.

Two receivers, both boosts

01The two boosts

Every flight in this report is one of two simulated boosts, flown straight up from the same launch site at the same time, under the same sky. The traveler follows USC's Traveler IV space shot: a 13 s burn that climbs from 6 to 19 g, 1,511 m/s at burnout and an apogee of 103.5 km. The hotshot is built to crowd the boost in below 515 m/s: its thrust ramps from 10 to 40 g in 4 s, so it passes 515 m/s at T+2.8 s already pulling 30 g, burns out at 878 m/s and coasts to 27.4 km.

Why the 515 m/s line matters here. Above it the NEO-M8T stops reporting raw measurements, and both receivers stop their fix, so it caps how much of a boost can be watched. The traveler spends 6.2 s of its burn below it, at 6 to 13 g; the hotshot spends 2.8 s, at 11 to 30 g. A soft start moves the whole flight by a tenth or two of a second and, in the PX1105R's check (section 05), changes no loss.

From acceleration to Doppler rate. A receiver feels the boost as its satellites' carriers sliding in frequency: 51.5 Hz/s for each g of the rocket's acceleration along the line to the satellite. In the burn that acceleration is 1 g less than the accelerometer reads, and each satellite gets the share its elevation sets. The steepest satellite in this sky, 66° up, is sliding at about 840 Hz/s at the traveler's burnout and 1,650 Hz/s at the hotshot's.

02The real sky

To see where a real sky sits on these ladders, both receivers logged the open sky through the same outdoor antenna for 20 minutes each, back to back on 29 September, with nothing transmitting and the bench's settings: SLR on the PX1105R, airborne <4 g on the NEO-M8T. Every mark is one GPS L1 C/A satellite's C/N0 over two minutes, as that receiver reports it.

A typical satellite sits in the middle of the ladders. The PX1105R reads the expected scale itself up to about 41 dBHz, so its GPS satellites were at 30–45, median 37. The NEO-M8T's readings, turned back through its bench calibration, put the same sky at about 31–43 above 15°. Either way the "sky-like" 38–41 dBHz used in this report is an ordinary satellite on this antenna, not a pessimistic choice, and the low ones are well below it: exactly the satellites that ride through a vertical boost best.

The other signals. On the PX1105R, Galileo E1 read a median of 35, BeiDou B1 41, and GPS L5 43; on the NEO-M8T, which tracks L1 only, Galileo 32.5 and GLONASS 35.5.

The two logs ran 25 minutes apart, so each satellite sat at a different elevation in each and single satellites do not compare; the elevation bands do. One antenna at one site; a small flight antenna on a rocket body is usually worse.

03At a glance

The companion report, GNSS Under Boost, showed that the satellites a receiver loses in a burn are the ones whose carrier is sliding fastest: the ones overhead, for a rocket going up. This report adds the other half of the question. How fast a slide a receiver can follow depends on how strong the signal is, so the same boost costs one receiver nothing and another most of its sky.

What the table says

The PX1105R has a Doppler-rate limit that rises with signal. Half its satellites are gone by 135–145 Hz/s at 35–38 dBHz, by about 330 Hz/s at 48, and at 75 dBHz, far above any sky, it lost nothing up to 840 Hz/s. One threshold per level predicts which satellite goes and when to within half a second. What it is really working to is its own speed, which trails the truth further the weaker the signal (section 05).

The NEO-M8T has a limit too, but it sits far away. It lost no satellite before its own cutoff on either boost until the signal reached 18 dBHz on the same scale, where it reads 29. Its reading runs 6 to 7 dB above the PX1105R's for the same signal, and at the same reading it follows a carrier three to eight times as fast (sections 06 and 07).

Same boost, same signal. On the hotshot at 35 to 41 dBHz the PX1105R keeps three satellites at best past T+2.8 s, and in three of four flights none, and no fix, past T+1.3 s. Fed the same files at 36 and 39, the NEO-M8T keeps all fourteen to its cutoff at T+2.8 s, the steepest sliding at 1,400 Hz/s (section 07).

04The bench and the levels

Both receivers were cabled to the same HackRF (transmit gain 3) through a fixed attenuator, 10 dB for the PX1105R (none for its 75 dBHz run) and 10, 20 or 30 dB for the NEO-M8T, and played files from the same simulator build, the same GPS ephemeris (18 Aug 2026, 08:30 UTC) and the same launch site, so every satellite has the same elevation and the same Doppler history in every run. Thirteen or fourteen GPS satellites are above the 3° mask on the pad. Only GPS L1 C/A is transmitted. Each receiver ran its most dynamic setting, set after a cold start on the pad and read back before every flight: the PX1105R in navigation mode 9, which SkyTraq calls SLR ("speed lag reduced"), raw measurements at 20 Hz; the NEO-M8T in its airborne <4 g model, GPS only, raw measurements at 18 Hz.

Signal level. Each level is a file scaled digitally in gps-sdr-sim, and it is quoted in "expected" dB-Hz: the scale calibrated on the PX1105R, where 65 dBHz is the full-scale file through 10 dB and every halving of the file's gain takes off 6 dB. The PX1105R reads that scale faithfully up to about 41 dBHz and flattens above it. The NEO-M8T reads its own number for the same signal, so its runs carry both:

Above about 44 the M8T's reading saturates at 48. Below that it runs 6–7 dB above the expected scale: its front end makes more of the same signal. The flights in the figures never scale the file below fixed gain 4, because the simulator's 8-bit output has too few steps left there to scale cleanly; the weakest levels come from more attenuation in the cable instead.

What counts as a loss. A satellite is lost when the receiver stops reporting a valid pseudorange for it for at least half a second, counted from ignition to half a second past burnout, so the jolt of the thrust cutting off is in. Its Doppler rate is the rocket's acceleration along the line to the satellite over the L1 wavelength: 51.5 Hz/s for each g, so a satellite overhead feels all of the boost and one on the horizon almost none.

One threshold predicts it. At 34.9 and 38.4 dBHz a single rate, 135 and 145 Hz/s, predicts the first nine of the thirteen losses to within half a second, in the order they happened. A satellite keeps reporting for about half a second after it crosses.

A second mechanism is tied to the receiver's own fix. Its position solution stops at the COCOM speed, between T+6.1 and T+6.8 s. At the weak levels the last satellites go with it, whatever their rate; at 45 and 48 the opposite happens and satellites come back. During the burn its own speed runs 10–140 m/s behind the truth, even in its speed-lag-reduced mode. The hotshot below shows how the two connect.

The 41 and 45 dBHz flights also carry a PX1105R bench effect: a receiver-internal cycle that drops most channels together every 11–13 seconds, but only while its fix runs and only at 40–45 dBHz. One or two of those land in each of those boosts, so those two columns overstate the loss. On the hotshot it struck the first 44 dBHz flight a second before ignition; the repeat shown was clean.

Is it the simulator?

Four checks say no. At 38.4 dBHz, a 0.25 s and a 0.5 s thrust ramp in place of the instant ignition, and the simulator's stock build with its stepped carrier, lose the same thirteen satellites in the same order at the same rates. A file where every satellite has its own strength, from path loss and an antenna pattern, puts each satellite's loss where the ladder says its own level should.

The hotshot

The PX1105R flew the same hotshot files as the NEO-M8T, through 10 dB of attenuation instead of 30, so its nine levels run from 59 down to 34.9 dBHz and meet the NEO-M8T's at the bottom. It lost satellites in the first second at every level. From 41 dBHz down, three of the four flights had lost all thirteen, and the fix, by T+1.3 s; the fourth, a repeat at 41, kept three until T+3.9 s.

Withheld, not lost, while its lag is small. At 59, 56 and 44 dBHz every satellite that went missing in the burn reappears in the raw output in the same 50 ms epoch, the first after the fix stops, with pseudoranges within about 20 m of the truth. The 45 and 48 dBHz travelers do the same. Satellites reacquired one by one would come back over seconds, so these were being tracked all along: the receiver stopped reporting them while its own solution disagreed with them. What decides whether they come back is not the signal but how far its own speed had fallen behind when the fix stopped at the COCOM speed. In every such flight on either boost where it was 86 m/s or less they returned (the 41 dBHz traveler, at 79, got most of them back); where it was 105 m/s or more they stayed gone. Where the fix ended earlier because no satellites were left, none came back.

The lag is where signal strength comes in. A satellite at 66° sees 50 m/s of lag as 240 Hz of Doppler the receiver is not expecting. Worked out per satellite from its own reported velocity and clock, that mismatch explains the traveler's weak levels: at 34.9 and 38.4 dBHz each of the first nine satellites dropped when its mismatch reached 42–78 Hz, centred near 60, at every elevation from 66° down to 16°, while their Doppler rates at the drop spanned nearly two to one. The strong-signal hotshot runs do not fit that simply: their first drops come at 15–45 Hz of mismatch, at the moment the receiver's own speed briefly stops climbing.

06NEO-M8T: nothing lost until the signal is very weak

The NEO-M8T stops reporting raw measurements, and its fix, above 515 m/s, so on the traveler only its first 6.3 s of boost are visible, with the steepest satellite at up to 565 Hz/s (11 g along its line). It lost nothing in that window at any level down to 21 dBHz. The hotshot was built to see more: 2.8 s of boost below the cutoff, with the steepest satellite sliding at up to 1,400 Hz/s.

Its limit depends on how long, not only how hard. At the same 18 dBHz the hotshot's satellites held 1,150–1,400 Hz/s for about three seconds, while on the traveler the steepest one went at 494 Hz/s after five. A threshold on rate alone does not describe this receiver.

Weak signal moves its cutoff. The COCOM check runs on the receiver's own speed, and that speed lags the truth more as the signal weakens: the cutoff that falls at T+2.8 s on a strong hotshot came at T+4.4 at 18 dBHz and T+9.7 at 15.

Where it stops. At 15 dBHz it fixes on eight satellites on the pad. At 12 it never gets a fix or a timed measurement in the whole flight, which ended the sweep.

08Next

A prediction model. For a given motor and launch time: each satellite's Doppler rate from the acceleration profile and its elevation, against the receiver's limit at that satellite's strength, gives when it drops. The PX1105R's single threshold already does this at the weak levels; the NEO-M8T needs the duration term. For the PX1105R the better input may be its own speed lag, since the Doppler mismatch it causes held near 60 Hz across elevations; that needs a model of how the lag grows with acceleration and signal.

Repeats at 41 to 45 dBHz. The PX1105R's bench cycle sits in the middle of an outdoor sky's range and hit three of the flights there; both hotshot repeats came out clean. The two flights at 41 also came out differently, one keeping a fix to the COCOM stop and one losing everything in the first 1.3 s, so that range wants several flights per level, and the traveler's 41 and 45 a clean repeat.

The PX1105R's navigation mode. It already flew in SLR, its lowest-lag setting. The same file in its airborne mode, which should lag more, is the direct test: if the losses come earlier and go deeper as its own speed falls further behind, the lag is what sets them, not the signal as such.

09What this does not cover

One sky, one launch site and one time. A conducted cable, so no antenna, body shadowing, spin or plume: each of those spends signal a real flight does not get back, which is exactly the margin these tables measure. GPS L1 C/A only, where the receivers normally track several constellations. The NEO-M8T's serial link stalled several times overnight; the capture tool now reopens it after a second of silence, and no stall fell in a boost.
