What a Rocket Boost Costs Hobby Grade GNSS Receivers

The simulated flights that measured where hobby-grade GNSS receivers stop publishing position also show what the boost itself does to them. Every flight replayed a byte-identical signal, so each satellite's measured Doppler applies to every receiver, and their tracking can be compared satellite by satellite. The loss follows how fast each satellite's carrier slides, not how far it sits off nominal. Five of the six keep tracking through a 13.5 g burn at the cost of carrier on their steepest satellites; the Quectel LC86G loses every channel at ignition and has no fix again until the descent.

Six receivers on the rate axis

01What the boost does

The companion report, GNSS Receiver Limits, is about the export gate: the moment a receiver refuses to publish a position it is perfectly capable of computing. This report is about a different kind of loss, and one that is not a policy decision at all. Under acceleration a receiver can stop tracking satellites, and which ones it loses is not random.

Every receiver below flew the same two boosts to the same 82.5 km apogee: a real high-power burn peaking at 13.5 g, and a gentle one peaking at 2.0 g. The companion report calls them the 15 g and 3 g flights; the figures here are the peak acceleration of the injected trajectory.

What the table says

The loss follows the Doppler rate. Which satellites fade is decided by how fast each one's carrier is sliding, and that is set by where it sits relative to the velocity vector: overhead suffers, near the horizon does not. How far the carrier sits off nominal does not predict it (section 03).

A g rating does not describe it. One acceleration presents a spread of rates across the sky, so a part flown under its rated limit is not automatically safe if something is overhead (section 04).

Five parts keep a fix; the LC86G does not. Losing three satellites out of thirteen costs the ZED-F9P geometry, not a position. The LC86G loses every channel at ignition, and the ones above its rate knee never come back during the burn (section 05).

02How this was measured

The rig is the one in the companion report. A HackRF plays a simulated GPS L1 C/A sky, made by gps-sdr-sim from the real broadcast ephemeris, into each receiver: by cable where possible, otherwise inside a sealed Faraday cage. Every satellite is transmitted at the same constant power for the whole flight, so a satellite that fades was faded by the receiver, not by the signal.

Two boosts, one sky. Both flights hold 180 s on the pad and then burn: 11.9 s peaking at 13.5 g, or 60.2 s peaking at 2.0 g. Every receiver on the rate axis flew the same two files, with the same ephemeris, start time and trajectory, so a given satellite presents the same Doppler to all of them at a given moment.

Measured Doppler, not modeled. The NEO-M8T logged RXM-RAWX, which reports its own Doppler per satellite in hertz. That gives two numbers per satellite: the rate, a least-squares slope of Doppler through the burn, which is what a tracking loop has to slew to follow; and the peak shift, how far off nominal the carrier sits. Because the files are byte-identical, the same numbers apply to every part, and doppler_ref.py --verify checks that identity before they are used.

Carrier loss is each satellite's carrier-to-noise through the burn against its own pad baseline, from each part's UBX NAV-SAT message. The SAM-M10Q's reaches the bench through its host computer's diagnostic console, once a second, placed in time by the receiver's own GPS time of week. The LC86G reports RTCM MSM7 instead: per-channel carrier-to-noise, its own Doppler and a carrier-phase lock counter, once a second. Each channel is set against the NEO-M8T's rate for the same satellite.

Only GPS L1 C/A is injected, and every part here is multi-constellation, so each is tracking fewer signals than it would in flight. In flight each would have more satellites to lose before its fix was at risk.

03Carrier loss follows the Doppler rate

Doppler rate is the suspect. An L1 carrier shifts 5.25 Hz for every m/s of range rate, so under acceleration the shift is not merely large, it is moving, and the tracking loop has to slew to follow it. How fast it moves depends on where the satellite sits relative to the velocity vector, which for a vertical boost means it scales with elevation. That gives two candidate explanations worth separating, because they are different failures: the carrier moving too fast for the loop, or the carrier simply sitting too far off nominal.

Carrier loss scales with the rate. Pooling both flights onto one axis makes the gentle one the low end of a single curve rather than a separate condition: its satellites never exceed 79 Hz/s and never lose carrier, while the same satellites on the same sky reach 501 Hz/s under a real boost and shed as much as 47 dB. Above 300 Hz/s the median satellite is down 13.5 dB; below 100 Hz/s the median is unchanged.

And it is the rate, not the shift. Peak shift is a genuine control here rather than a straw man, because it is close to independent of elevation — it is set mostly by the satellite's own motion, and it ranges from 1.2 to 6.9 kHz across this sky with no relation to how hard the loop is working. GPS 5 sits at 28° carrying the largest shift of any satellite, 6915 Hz, and barely suffers. 04A g rating for a Hz/s problem

The published limit is a g number for a Hz/s problem. Datasheets rate dynamics in g: u-blox gives its M10 generation 4 g, and Quectel gives the LC86G the same. But a loop does not see acceleration, it sees how fast its carrier is sliding — and the same 4 g produces 206 Hz/s on a satellite at zenith and 71 Hz/s on one at 20°. One acceleration is therefore not one condition; it is a spread. That is visible in the 13.5 g flight, which holds 3.4 times the rated limit at its peak: split its satellites at the 4 g-equivalent rate and the ones below it are down 3.5 dB while the ones above are down 7.5 dB — same vehicle, same instant, differing only in where they sit in the sky. A part flown past its rated acceleration does not fail all at once, and a part kept under it is not automatically safe if something is overhead.

The rating can also be conservative. The SAM-M10Q, a u-blox M10 module, held every satellite within 5 dB of its pad level up to 312 Hz/s, half again the 206 Hz/s its 4 g implies overhead. It lost 13 dB at 378 Hz/s and 35 dB at 501.

So the mechanism has a name: the tracking loop failing to slew, not the carrier sitting far off nominal. Elevation, which this analysis first used to argue from, turns out to have been a sound proxy rather than a wrong one — the measured rate matches a × sin(elevation) × 5.25 to within a factor of 0.82 to 0.98 across twelve satellites, the residual being the difference between peak and mean acceleration over the burn. That is why it worked.

The clearest single observation is a satellite that disappears. GPS 11 sits at 70°, the highest in this sky and the steepest Doppler rate of any satellite in the set. Its raw measurement holds steady at −98 Hz on the pad; one second after ignition it reads +483 Hz and its carrier has fallen from 48 to 30 dBHz; then it vanishes from the raw output entirely for ten seconds, and returns at +6302 Hz with its carrier back at 47 dBHz the moment the burn ends. It is bracketed by the acceleration on both sides. It is also, for that reason, the one satellite absent from the rate axis: the instrument that would measure the stimulus is the one the stimulus disabled.

What actually dropped. Of these five parts, only the ZED-F9P lost satellites outright, three of thirteen: GPS 11 at 70°, GPS 24 at 51° and GPS 6 at 31°, each from a healthy carrier to nothing. The other four leaned on the same satellites — 12 to 35 dB off the two steepest — but held lock on all of them; the SAM-M10Q's GPS 24 hung on at 5 dBHz. The ranking is consistent across parts even where the outcome is not.

What did not drop, on these five, is the position. None of them went to no-lock during either burn. Losing three satellites out of thirteen costs geometry, not a fix. On these five parts, the thing that stops a position is the export gate, not the dynamics.

Three of the five parts behind these curves are u-blox modules, the NEO-M8T, the SAM-M10Q and the ZED-F9P; the Quescan and the Beitian speak the same UBX interface. The LC86G in section 05 comes from another vendor and fails differently. What makes the result credible is five receivers agreeing, the effect scaling with acceleration, and the rate beating the shift on every one of them.

05The LC86G loses the position

The Quectel LC86G reports its raw measurements as RTCM MSM7: per-satellite C/N₀, its own Doppler, and a carrier-phase lock counter, once a second. It was flown on the same byte-identical files, so each of its channels can be set against the Doppler rate the NEO-M8T measured for the same satellite. It fails in two stages. At the 13.5 g ignition every channel drops within the first second, low and high alike. That is a common-mode failure, not per-channel stress, and it happens in all three navigation modes flown. Then the rate-limited picture reasserts itself. The channels whose Doppler rate is low relock within two seconds and hold through the rest of the burn, and the others do not.

The knee sits between 128 and 171 Hz/s. That is below the 206 Hz/s its rated 4 g would present straight overhead, so a satellite near zenith is lost somewhere around 3 g of sustained acceleration. With only the four lowest satellites left, all under 13°, it has no fix for the rest of the burn and coast. The 2 g flight sits under the knee everywhere, and the channels show it. All thirteen held 46 dBHz through the burn, and only the three steepest, at 79, 66 and 53 Hz/s, slipped carrier phase at ignition. That is the same ranking the other five parts show in carrier loss, visible here in phase, at an acceleration too low to cost any carrier at all.

So the LC86G is the one part on this bench whose fix the boost costs. It is not the export gate, which it never reached; it is the dynamics alone, at ignition. On a vehicle that boosts that hard it will not have a position until the descent, whatever its navigation mode.

Its navigation mode is a second, separate failure. Everything above is Balloon mode. In the module's power-up default, Normal mode, on the gentle flight, the navigation solution did not follow the climb: for 18 s it reported a climb rate of 1–6 m/s with a valid 3-D fix from all 13 satellites while the vehicle reached 320 m/s. That is the navigation filter, not the tracking loops, and the companion report sets the modes side by side (GNSS Receiver Limits, section 04). After the 13.5 g ignition Normal mode went further, publishing fixes flagged valid that were 18–80 km wrong. Nothing in those fixes marks them as bad, so a flight computer that trusts the valid flag takes them as they are, including one that falls back on GNSS altitude to deploy a main parachute.

Drone mode, flown on a later day, relocked nothing during the burn. It lost every channel at the 13.5 g ignition like the other two modes, but its raw measurements carry no channel at all from ignition until 242 s, 50 s after burnout, where Balloon mode had its four low satellites back within 2 s. That run's signal was about 3 dB weaker on the pad, 42 against 45 dBHz, so it does not separate the mode from the signal level. On the gentle flight Drone mode followed the climb as closely as Balloon mode, but its altitude was about a second old: 0.45 km low at 496 m/s.

06What this bench cannot show

One boundary decides how far this generalizes. The rig injects smooth, clean, interpolated motion. A real motor adds broadband vibration that modulates carrier phase directly, plume attenuation, body shadowing and antenna-pattern nulls as the vehicle rolls, and a signal level well below the 32–51 dBHz delivered here — and loop stress multiplies with low carrier. A loop that holds 500 Hz/s at 45 dBHz can fail at 32. A part that sails through boost here may still drop lock on a real motor, and nothing measured here contradicts that.

The rate axis is also one sky: the GPS satellites visible from one site at one hour, the steepest at 70°. A different sky puts different satellites overhead, and the ranking follows the sky, not the satellite numbers.
